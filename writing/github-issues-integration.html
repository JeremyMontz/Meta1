GitHub Issues integration

Backlog management, minus the bottleneck.

Every new idea, every issue, shows up when you’re working on something else, usually at the worst moment to stop and write it down. For the past month my only options were to break focus and capture it, or lose it to time.

In v2 I tracked all of it by hand. I stood up Bond, my product-owner and quality-control agent, to “manage” the list of tasks in a spreadsheet: bugs, open threads, priorities. Agents could not write their own notes, so I became the relay and copied and pasted them everywhere myself. I had so many, I couldn’t build and fix everything all at once. So I started planning versions, and Bond was meant to help me gate new releases for quality. It worked, barely. It was questionably worth doing, but what choice did I have?

A backlog needs a product owner. It stopped needing a dedicated one.

The integration came in stages. It started as an experiment in v2.5, a proof of concept that worked end to end, printing its first issue on the board. But I was busy building v2.6, the agentic theater that later collapsed on itself. Once we moved to Cowork and a local disk in v3.0, I immediately built these GitHub skills to capture ephemeral notes, the working L5 memory layer. Two custom skills, one to create new issues and one to query the open task list, usually at session start. Now I just say it: “log a bug for the truncation thing,” or “write a story that captures these new requirements.” It files or pulls mid-conversation, without me leaving my chain of thought.

I didn’t need to pay the specialization tax of keeping a separate agent devoted to doing this task properly; now it was a skill any agent could run. I put Bond on hiatus. It will return, but not until I can automate its evals and use-case tests. For now I will enjoy the feeling of telling agents to do the work I get paid to do in my day job. Two months and over >225 issues later, this has been the proof that the v3 vision is paying off.
