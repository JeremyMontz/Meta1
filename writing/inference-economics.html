Inference economics.

Scaling a skill while watching the meter.

My first real long-running job was feeding my favorite book into the wiki, my second brain. Nearly twelve hundred pages. It was the first time I noticed inference getting capped out, often at the most inconvenient times.

The custom skillset I was building had to scale, and to recover gracefully. I started paying closer attention when fixing one thing made a ten-page batch swallow four hours of capacity in twenty minutes, and just like that the limit was real. So I ran it in small batches, each one checked against an approved sample, the token meter open beside me. The useful insight was that quality and cost moved independently. The wrong improvement could blow up the math, turning a job that should cost ten sessions into hundreds of multi-hour ones. When a fix made the output worse, the meter told me to keep iterating, that a cheaper path still existed, and a bad batch caught early saved a whole expensive run later.

Cost wasn’t just a bill. It was a calibration signal.

It was the first time since going Max that I’d seen my usage cut off, and it moved my timelines. I was monitoring my inference in real time, learning to feel the difference between what was spent well and what was wasted. I later learned the ledger has a name, inference economics, the same accounting every production system eventually runs into. And this project let me feel it viscerally. Using cost as a calibration signal, I finalized the skill, and eventually the job ran predictably to the end on budget.
