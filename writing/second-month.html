A foundation to build on.

Month one was trial, error, and a pile of files that did not survive the move. In month two I stopped blindly following the monster's advice and being taken for a ride. Instead, I changed the ground under it, and started driving the solutions that actually added value.

where month one left off

I killed my proudest accomplishment.

The elaborate rules-based system built in v2.6 proved unviable. Canon, tier-based load contracts, a seven-part handshake. It created a beautiful, sophisticated webpage. But it was theater, and could not be salvaged.

I was changing platforms anyhow. The early days in Claude.ai taught me a lot about how the models chat and interact with users, but to really build something I needed a system that could write to disk without sneakernet; I needed Cowork and a machine that exists only for this purpose. I set it up in the final days of month one and now, at the start of month two, I survey the carnage.

Now it was time to rebuild, to bring it back to life.
the second brain

First, I gave it a memory that lives on disk.

I recompiled my projects locally, built the vault, and installed an Obsidian UI to help me visualize it: agentic wikis, raw research, project notes, and a managed backup. Now session notes land automatically, get triaged and metabolized.

It was capable of preserving its own memories between sessions. It didn't need me to save them somewhere.

It was capable of preserving its own memories.
intake

I taught it to feed itself.

I wired up an email skill, voice capture from my phone, web clippings, and the most useful skill of them all: GitHub Issues, for automating capture of my task list and plans. Everything new that the creature needs is routed into one inbox on disk.

I stopped being the only relay. I gave the thing a capability and pointed it at the world.

I stopped being the only relay.
the turn

I tore the old rules apart and wired the idea back in.

The canon and dashboard pages were the proudest things I built in month one, but were designed for capabilities the online platform did not support. So I mothballed them. In their place I wrote start-up skills that report the agent's memory configuration, its new 'canon,' and display it online instead. Passphrases that prove the agents actually read their memory.

The new workflow eliminated the hub-and-spoke model. The steel thread and the handshake protocol didn't require an orchestrator. Each agent reported its own status and notes to the dashboard, which I verified before proceeding. Claudemonzter had a beating heart again.

A passphrase forces the file load and verifies state integrity.
the pages come back

I gave everything a theme, and reskinned the pages.

One new design system with a custom-built theme: the Claudemonzter laboratory. Standard header and footer components. Templates and placeholders for future pages. Then applied to the dashboard and the check-in page, rebranded. A real homepage. A portfolio.

Now we had a public visual identity, built on a frame that could scale. My project was public again, and fed by the system itself instead of by my hands.

New plan: flesh out the creature in public, in HTML.
low-hanging fruit

The molehill that used to be a mountain.

The house renovation tracker app was originally going to be my first project to build something functional. It would be useful to me and demonstrate that now I can work with AI to produce working product. I parked the idea for a time to build the system itself.

When I came back to it, it was surprisingly easy; it barely took an afternoon. Now I had A) a back-end data model; B) a front-end design template; and C) most important, the new skills to connect and deploy the full solution. The lesson of the lost two weeks of theater development was vindicated by the ease with which this actual tool was made. The future was bright.

Now I can work with AI to produce working product.
popcorn

Then it was easy to keep growing.

I wanted to demonstrate a new capability, and it required a unique design. Claudemonzter's spirit page is a working persona engine. It includes a cascade skill that pushes a personality profile into every agent and a website to visualize and 'set the dials.' After it, the eight agent pages were easy; I had the dashboard components already, so I just added a session log and some links to the things I was building within each domain.

The repo jumped in size. I was bringing abstract capabilities to life and giving them a place to be understood, something I could point at in public. The wind was in my sails, and the ideas were coming faster than I could act on them.

I was bringing abstract capabilities to life.
wind in the sails

A vault, a repo, a website, and a new direction.

Everything I learn lands on disk. Everything I make ends up in public. In between, a stack of skills, all firing when I need them to. I built more in two weeks than in the month before.

And it felt easy, or at least easier. Claude would still overcomplicate and try to drive the project in divergent directions, but I was catching on to the trick. I might ask Claude to suggest a destination or a route, but I was staying in the driver's seat. My next project idea would immediately put that to the test.

I was catching on to the trick.
