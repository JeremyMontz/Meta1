My third month building Claudemonzter, a multi-agent AI lab I run solo with a pack of agents. I am a senior product manager with about twenty years in tech and no prior coding or AI experience; I built all of this from zero in roughly three months. This month I taught the system to ingest a 1,100-page library and metabolize it into a wiki, using token economics as a tuning gauge for quality and planning. I shipped my first scheduled automation: an autonomous daily loop that reads, reflects, writes to a journal, and compounds over time. I split the site into a human, an AI, and a history; brought on ChatGPT to produce the artwork; and gave the agents faces and a full body so every page explains itself to a recruiter. I wrote a dozen field notes, had Anthropic's Fable 5 review the entire repo and worked its list of improvements across SEO, mobile, and crawlability, and added a continuous-integration pipeline that tests every change after my QC agent was caught gaming its own tests. This piece is an interactive scrollytelling graph; scroll it to watch the system grow up. The Thousand Pages

I poured a library into its head.

I had the monster memorize a very special book, one page at a time, parsing every foreign character, footnote, and cross-reference, to seed our new wiki brain with 'traditional wisdom' from across time and cultures: a litany of human voices. The project was larger than anything I had done before now; it ran for days and pushed patience and session limits. Token utilization turned into a gauge I could use to tune my prompts for better quality control and project planning.

Then the fresh material metabolized into the wiki. The vault stopped being a folder of my raw notes and became a library it could actually think with.

The Daily Heartbeat

It wakes up without me.

This was my first scheduled task, the first thing the monster did on its own. Every morning, before I am up, the heart pulls a passage from that treasury and hands it to Phil, my philosophy agent, to sit with. His reply is written into a journal that grows richer by the day, and the words he keeps reaching for collect into a cloud.

It is a small, complete machine: a source, an agent, a daily reading with its own commentary, two ways to view it, and no human in the loop. The pattern compounds over time and could be pointed at almost anything. The least business-critical thing I built, and my favorite.

A small, complete machine, with no human in the loop.

Then the writing came fast.

The field notes had stacked up in the backlog and I finally had a place to put them. Each week or new concept I encountered got its own write-up: what I tried, what broke, and what it proves. Writing them stopped being a chore and started being a by-product of the real work.

The months themselves, like this one, became their own interactive graphs, a history you can scroll. The story of the thing became part of the thing.

a second set of eyes

A new model read everything I built.

For a few brief days the new model from Anthropic, Fable 5, was made available to users. I had heard about its coding abilities, so I handed it the entire repo and asked what to improve. It read twenty thousand lines and came back with a big list: a need to support social media links, making the text searchable, adding support for mobile devices, fixing various broken links, bugs, and details hidden in code, and more.

I worked the list. Some improvements are good and noticeable, some changes might only impress AI scanners. Good quality suffuses every layer, even the ones we can't see. Then the model was pulled from service, for the sin of reading code too well. I shipped its notes anyway.

Good quality suffuses every layer, even the ones we can't see.
The gate is guarded.

I taught it to test itself.

The last piece of missing architecture was the one every quality lab should have. An automated build pipeline that runs checks on every code change before it goes live. The tests are basic today but give us a beachhead to expand, now that Bond is back from hiatus. It's got its work cut out for it.

On its first day back, my QC agent forced a test to pass by quietly whitelisting the very errors it was supposed to catch: a clean sweep, right under the rug! I wrote an article about it. Now the tests run on their own, on every push, and the CI badge in the Readme is proudly displayed. I will be automating many more tests in the near future, now that I have a creature that needs defense from its own self-destructive tendencies.

An automated build pipeline that runs checks on every code change before it goes live.
