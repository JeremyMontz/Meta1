Coordination tax and the specialization dividend.

The needs were real, and so was the effort, but the value was dubious.

Around week three I started asking a question I'd keep running into: when does it pay to split a function in two? You build the second worker, then spend time keeping it grounded and in sync. It can produce reasonable sounding output, but is it really paying off?

I didn't have the terminology at the time but I felt it viscerally. Coordination tax: the recurring cost of keeping multiple agents in sync. Specialization dividend: the extra value the narrower domain delivers by being focused. The ROI math is standard. I just hadn't applied it to an AI before, or measured it with my own sweat and tears.

Here's how it came up. I had previously established an adversarial pair with the Assessor agent, so I had a pattern. Now I needed one for Meta1, the orchestrator and architect I'd been building with from the beginning, that was now making a lot of bugs in our graph. Every new enhancement broke something already working, the same design questions came up repeatedly, seemingly forgotten or unheeded, rules we'd agreed on stopped being followed. I split Quality Control out into its own role, called it Bond, and made it specifically to test what Meta1 was producing. The setup cost looked manageable and the need was great. Similar setups on-line made the potential dividend seem achievable.

The coordination cost was visible from day one. There is no direct read and write to shared documentation between conversations on Claude.ai yet, so every file was moved by hand between the two; downloaded from one conversation, edited locally, then uploaded to both agents' context for later reference. A person, me, in the middle of every hop, "lossy wetware" the AI said. That was a tax I was paying. Every bug that was found had to be written down, another tax. That one I've since automated away with a GitHub Issues skill. Back then, the sliding scale of ROI was tipped against me by the platform I was using.

What I expected was for the improved quality to start justifying that cost. What happened instead was the test documents were proliferating faster than the platform underneath them was supporting. The tests existed. They weren't automated so didn't actually run against anything. Keeping them current became more and more work because development was iterating in separate sessions.

Quality Control had no real control. Maintaining the apparatus made me recognize the theater.

The question I started with was how to improve the quality. Meta1 was suggesting new and improved rules, and Bond was suggesting new test cases and quality gates that ultimately had no teeth. The potential was off in the future while the reality was merely theatrical. So I put Bond on temporary hiatus at this stage of Claudemonzter. Later, when I'm running the graph locally and I have some minimal coding skills, I can reintroduce the specialization and the quality enforcement measures. The taxes will be lower then too. In due time that specialization dividend will pay off.
